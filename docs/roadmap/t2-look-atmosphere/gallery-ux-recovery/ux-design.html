Lighting visual language

proposed design · refines #314 · owner review

How Biskiq draws light, and how a World is lit before anyone adds a light. Icons, the emitter in the Stage, the beam guide, the daylight and night setup, future source shapes, and one icon language for the Card and Sky controls. It is the design target for the later lighting slices (S2 Sky, S3 follow-ups, G1, S7, S8) that have no UX design of their own. Rules it would replace are listed in §9 and are not edited here.

The rule that holds everything together: guides are lines, only light is bright. A guide shows where light can reach; only the rendered picture shows what it does. Guides are drawn in line only, in the Stage's own inks: ink for an aim, leaf green on hover, a gold edge when selected, each on a thin white halo. No fill, no gradient, no glow, never the light's own colour, so selecting a light never changes how the room looks. The Sun has no guide at all: its shadows show where it is. Only the render may glow.

1 · FixtureThe visible object: a can, a tube, a shade. Part of the Scene, chosen from the Library, grouped with its light. S8 · G1
2 · EmitterThe logical light: the Scene light entity with position, direction and shape. Visitors never see it. Point · Spot authored; the Sun is the Sky's
3 · Stage markWhat the editor draws so the emitter can be found, read and handled: glyph, guides, grips. Derived and never serialized. this section
4 · LightThe rendered result: illumination, falloff, shadows. This is the only layer that ever looks like light. renderer

A · Instrument shipped base

The shell's open 1.5 px forms. Today's Lights tool is a bulb; Brightness, Colour, Shape and Shadows are the Advanced group heads shipped in 0923555e.

Keep: grid, stroke and rhythm. Problems: the bulb is a fixture; Brightness and Sun are the same glyph (shell-icons.ts lightBrightness = sun); Spot looks like Shape; nothing in the set grows into line or panel sources.

B · Aperture recommended

Every light kind is drawn as its emitter shape plus how light leaves it: rays all round for a point, an aperture for a spot, parallel arrows for distant light, a bar or panel for extended sources.

Why: the toolbar, Library, Index, Card and Stage glyph all use the same forms; the Sun and Brightness collision goes away; it grows naturally into line and panel sources; and it keeps Instrument's 24 grid and 1.5 round stroke. Cost: the Point rays still look like a generic "brightness" glyph outside Biskiq.

C · Fixture rejected

Pictures of lamps: bare bulb, track can, batten, troffer, pendant; dimmer, gel, barn doors.

Instantly familiar to lighting people, but it makes the emitter look like a fixture, which is the confusion this brief is meant to prevent. Once S8 fixture models exist, the Library would show a "Pendant" light icon next to a Pendant object. The heavier strokes also clash with the 1.5 px shell.

D · Photometric later, Advanced only

Polar intensity curves, like an IES data sheet: a circle for a point, a lobe for a spot, a batwing for a pendant.

Accurate and distinctive, and it would carry IES data later. At 16 px the Spot lobe reads as a pin or a drop and the Point is just a circle. Worth keeping for one future Distribution group head, not as the main family.

Should it always be a point?

  • Point and Spot stay screen-space glyphs of constant size (20 px, fix.md §4.2). The emitter really is a point, and its glyph has to stay readable at any zoom.
  • The emitter is a small object, drawn like the Stage's Camera: white faces, one ink outline and a soft contact shadow, so it reads as a thing you can pick up, not as a cursor. A Point is a glass sphere with rays all round; a Spot is a short lens can turned to its aim, in place of today's filled wedge, which reads as a play button.
  • Only the lens or core carries the light's colour, as a flat swatch with a white glint. It never glows, so the render stays the only light, and you can see a light's colour before it is lit. It is an abstract emitter, not a lamp: no shade, no socket, no fixture picture.
  • Extended sources are drawn at world size, because their length or area is authored: a capsule for a line, an outlined panel with an inset and a facing tick for a rectangle. They are outline and white field, never a filled bright shape.
  • The Sun has no Stage glyph and no beam. It has no position; its shadows show it, and its mark is the Sky's sundial on the floor (§4).

A · Footprint first recommended

Source glyph, aim line and the ring where the beam lands, projected onto the surfaces it meets. The cone's wire boundaries appear only while Beam or Soft edge is dragged.

Why: the footprint answers the question a creator asks, "what does this light hit?", and it lies on the surface, so nothing fills the air. Three marks at rest. Cost: the angle shows only while you edit it; the Card's Beam readout covers it at rest. Stage: screen-space lines on a white halo, depth-tested against the scene; no blend state, no post pass.

B · Falloff contours

The footprint drawn as three lines of equal strength: full brightness, half, and the edge. The same aim line as A; no cone, ever.

Why: the soft edge reads as a lighting-plan gradient of lines, with no fill. Cost: three rings per light get busy in a room of many lights, and the half line promises a precision the WebGL render does not keep.

C · Wire frustum

The Camera's own wire language: lines from the source to the footprint, the silhouette edges and the footprint ring, whenever the light is selected.

Why: lights and Cameras read as one family of projectors, and the angle is always visible. Cost: the most lines; a wire cone at rest competes with Camera frustums and reads like a View.

Superseded and rejected: sage glass, a hue-only tinted cone: the tint reads as coloured haze and the cone fills the air. Ink veil: it darkens the room. Engraved hatching and light lines: both read as beams of light. Glowing glass: the mark imitates the light. #314 Instrument (dashed rings and edges always drawn): replaced; its ring returns only while dragging. The earlier landing trace is now the footprint ring, and the protractor became the fold-up fan.

Every kind, one guide

The same room six times, drawn by the same renderer in look A. Future kinds are labelled; none is a control today.

4 · Daylight and night a beautiful default, then authoring

Biskiq's principle: a World is beautifully lit before anyone places a light. Open a room at 13:00 and the Sun comes through the windows, picks the side away from the artwork and lights the space. Sky popover · the Sky chip on the toolbar

The Sun belongs to the Sky

One Sun (or Moon) per World. It is never placed, never in the Index and never in the Lights shelf. It has no Stage glyph and no beam: its shadows show it. Sunlight comes through the windows with the mullions' shadows in it, and the floor mark is a sundial: a pin whose shadow points away from the Sun and grows as it sinks, with a rayed sun or a crescent moon above it, shown while the Sky popover is open.

Direction: Auto. The Sun's bearing is solved from the openings so light falls through the windows and away from the artwork, shown as Auto. Editing it, or dragging the sunlight or the shadow, shows Auto · edited with Reset. Time is a mood dial: it sets height and colour (elevation 4° to 62° between 06:30 and 19:30; the moon at 38° from 21:00 to 04:30), not a real clock or location.

Window light and night are derived

Each opening becomes a soft source of sky light: a sky portal. Where there are no openings, daylight dims to a low ambient level instead of leaking through walls. At night the same openings carry moonlight or city glow.

One stored number: Window light strength. Everything else is computed from the Sky and the Layout, so it follows edits to openings and is never a second set of lights (hard rule 3). The Sun always casts shadows: without them a WebGL directional light passes straight through walls.

Dark rooms get help, not lights

When the inside is dark (night, or no openings), the Stage offers two quiet pills. Suggest lights applies a Recipe: ordinary Point and Spot lights you can then edit, aimed at the artworks. Use work light brightens the editor view only.

Nothing is added without a click. The work light is editor-only, never saved and never seen by visitors; it carries the dotted frame of the editor-only icons (§6). Night intensities are tuned for mood, so a Moonlit room reads as night, not as a dim day.

Legacy Directional lights

Existing Directional entities keep rendering exactly as they do. They appear in the Index as Extra parallel light · legacy, with a dotted-frame icon. Their Card offers Make this the sun, which copies From and Height into the Sky and removes the extra light in one undoable step.

Directional leaves the Lights shelf, so no new ones are created and a World never has two suns. The #314 multi-Directional dial is replaced by the Sky's single dial.

Render tiers · the renderer realises intent

The creator authors intent (Time, Direction, Window light, lights). How the renderer achieves it is never stored, so WebGPU can improve the picture without a format change. While dragging, the editor uses the fast path, then refines on release (Refining light…).

7 · Recommended direction

A World lit by default, with guides that never pretend to be light

  1. Kind icons: family B, Aperture, on Instrument's grid (24, 1.5 round).
  2. Beam guide: footprint first for every light: the source glyph, an aim line and the ring where the beam lands, in line only. The wire cone and the #314 ring appear only while a width grip is held; a fold-up fan takes over when the footprint is tiny or off-screen. The Sun has no guide: its shadows show it.
  3. The Sun belongs to the Sky: one per World, Auto direction solved from the openings, a sundial floor mark, draggable sunlight and shadow, Time as a mood dial. Directional leaves the Lights shelf; legacy ones keep working and can become the Sun.
  4. Window light and night are derived from the Sky and the openings, with one stored strength. Dark rooms get Suggest lights and an editor-only work light, never automatic lights.
  5. Interior lights are authored: Point and Spot, by hand or from a Recipe.
  6. Controls: the eight-primitive icon language; ink icons, colour only in values; a warmth track and a colour wheel on the Card.
  7. Render tiers: WebGL is the floor, WebGPU improves the same intent, releases bake. Nothing about the technique is stored.

Trade-offs accepted: the beam angle shows on the Stage only while it is edited (the Card readout covers it at rest); Auto direction can surprise a creator who expects a compass, so the dial always shows the bearing and Reset; derived window light needs a budget (the N largest openings) on WebGL.

Rejected: glowing guides, or guides in the light's own colour; a tinted cone (sage glass) and an ink veil; engraved hatching and light lines (they read as beams); beams for the Sun; several Directional lights as suns; automatic interior lights at night; a real-clock Time with latitude; storing a renderer technique in the World.
