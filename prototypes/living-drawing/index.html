An interaction model for original form · Independent commission

Living Drawings

Draw where it goes. Pull it into being. Draw over it to change your mind. The room is the drawing board, and every form stays a drawing you can keep changing.

In Biskiq you never edit geometry. A form is a plan drawn on the space and a profile drawn against it. Whatever it touches on purpose becomes a visible relationship. The form is re-made from those drawings every time anything changes. So it adapts when the room changes, explains how it was made, and has nothing fragile to break.

The challenge, reframed

Making inside a place, then changing your mind

Most of what people invent in Biskiq belongs somewhere. A pedestal belongs to its sculpture. A bench belongs to the wall it runs along, and a frame to the opening it surrounds. A cornice belongs to the line where the wall meets the ceiling. Their shape depends on what is around them.

Conventional modelling starts from an empty workplane and treats that surrounding context as something to measure and copy in. A snap lands a point on a wall and then forgets the wall. When the room changes, the object doesn't. History trees try to remember intent, but they remember the order of operations, not what the object is for, and they break when an early face disappears.

So the question for Biskiq is not which modelling operations to offer. It is how a creator can say what a form is for, in this place, using a gesture as quick as a sketch, and keep saying it as the place and their ideas change.

The language

Three gestures, the same at minute one and hour ten

Draw on

Put a plan on a surface, along a seam, or around an object. Closed strokes become masses or recesses. Open strokes become runs.

Pull

Grow a plan out of its host, or push it in. Pushing into a wall makes a niche: a subtraction allowed only into a host, never a free boolean.

Draw over

Redraw any edge you can see: the side of a pedestal, the outline of a niche, the section of a bench. Only the stretch you drew over changes.

The context reads your stroke

The creator never picks a tool for each case. A label at the pen tip says what the stroke is about to mean before they let go, for example “Following the floor seam” or “Around the sculpture”. The seam lights up while they trace it.

The representation

Every form is a plan and a profile

This is how architects and joiners already think: a plan says where, a section or elevation says how it rises. Biskiq keeps exactly those two drawings and makes the form from them.

  • Mass: a closed plan × an elevation profile, which says how far the plan's edge moves in or out as it rises. A straight profile gives a plinth, a waisted one a pedestal, a circular plan a vessel.
  • Run: a path × a section. The bench along the wall, the cornice, the frame around a niche, a shelf.
  • Recess: a plan on a wall, pushed in.

Context closes the shape. You only draw the line you would see. For a bench you draw from the wall down to the floor, and the wall and floor complete the section. For a frame you draw from the wall face to the niche edge. Profiles are defined against those context lines rather than in absolute space. That is why one moulding can serve as a frame around a niche and as a cornice under the ceiling.

Relationships

Snaps are free. Ties are visible, and yours.

A snap is help in the moment. The stroke straightens, closes, or lands exactly on the wall. It leaves nothing behind.

A tie is a dependency: this form will change when that thing changes. Biskiq only makes a tie from a gesture that clearly says so, such as tracing a seam, looping around an object, or pulling a height onto the ceiling. Contact that might be incidental, like a stroke ending on the far wall, becomes a proposed tie. It shows as a hollow knot, and one tap ties it.

Every tie has a name in plain words, a knot on the form, a line in the form's card, and an Untie. The Relations view (R, or hold Alt) dims the room and draws a labelled thread from each form to whatever it depends on. Grab a wall to move it, and the threads to everything tied to that wall light up before you move anything.

Understanding a form later

The form tells its own story

Select a form and its recipe reads as a sentence. Every bold word is a control. Something switched off stays in the sentence, struck through, so you can bring it back. There is no history tree, because the order you did things in doesn't matter. What matters is what the form is and what it depends on.

Revision without loss

Nothing changes silently

Indirect change is always narrated

When you change the room, or change a form that others depend on, every form that responded says how and why. Forms that didn’t respond say why too, if they touched what moved. While you drag a wall or the ceiling, white outlines show where things were.

Slack, never redirect

If a tie’s target disappears, the form keeps its last shape. Its knot turns to dashed gold and its card asks what to do: keep it as it is, remove it, or follow something you choose. It never jumps to the nearest similar thing.

Switched off, not deleted

A shaped profile can be switched off and on. Undo covers everything. The drawings are always still there to edit.

Spontaneous to sustained

Fifteen minutes, then ten hours

There is no beginner mode and no expert mode. The gestures you learn in the first minute are the ones you use at hour ten, just more precisely. Depth comes from more relationships and more refined drawings, never from a second interface.

Minute 1

Loop, pull. A pedestal that fits the sculpture, at eye level.

Minute 15

A bench along the wall and around the corner, a niche with a frame, a cornice. Tidy turns strokes into clean lines and curves.

Hour 1

Draw over sides and sections. Swap the sculpture, widen the room, read the notes. Tie what should follow; leave the rest alone.

Hour 3

Drag control points, type exact values, toggle corners and curves. Share one moulding across frame and cornice.

Hour 10

A small library of the room’s own profiles. Decide which forms follow the architecture and which hold still, and edit each profile once for every form that uses it.

The precision ladder

Loose keeps the character of the hand. Tidy, the default, finds corners, straightens near-horizontal and near-vertical lines, recognises circles and closes loops, and every result stays editable. Exact is a number typed into the sentence or while pulling, and the unit appears with it: a real 10 cm scale bar in the profile lens, metres in the room.

Native depth, assets welcome

Native for what belongs to the place

Native forms in Biskiq are the ones that only make sense in their place: pedestals, plinths, niches and openings, mouldings and frames, built-in benches, shelves, rails and reliefs. That is where context-aware drawing pays off.

Sculpture, complex furniture and organic objects should arrive as assets, imported or generated. In this model they become context. You can fit to them, frame them and rest them on things, and the native forms keep answering to them when they are swapped or moved. In the prototype the sculpture is a stand-in asset with three variants of different sizes.

AI fits as a proposer: it can suggest a drawing, a profile or an asset. It never makes opaque edits to native forms, because anything it proposes arrives as drawings and ties the creator can read and change.

Interface

The canvas leads; colour carries meaning

The room fills the window. A forest-green bar holds the instruments: Look, Draw, Edit the space, and Tidy or Loose. Cards appear only when there is something to read: the recipe of the selected form, the profile lens, the notes after a change. Editing the space is a separate mode on purpose, so changing the room never feels like editing your work.

Honest account

What building it taught us

Contextual ties and the plan × profile representation went in as hypotheses. These findings come from building the prototype and deliberately pushing awkward cases through it.

Held

The context can read a stroke

Four contexts cover the three journeys without a tool palette: along a seam, around an object, closed on a wall, and open on a wall. The label at the pen tip turned out to be essential. Without it the interpretation feels like magic, and magic is hard to trust.

Changed

Touching the far wall no longer ties

The first version tied a bench to the right wall whenever the stroke ended there. Following the research guidance, that contact is now only proposed. The result is more instructive: when the room widens, the bench stays put and says why, and the creator decides. Narrating non-responses was something we hadn't planned. It turned out to matter as much as narrating responses.

Held

One kernel, many forms

The pedestal, bench, frame, cornice and shelf all come from one profiled sweep. A moulding drawn for the frame works unchanged as the cornice, because sections are defined against context lines rather than world axes.

Broke

Things plan × profile can’t say

A bench with an armrest at only one end. A pedestal whose front differs from its side. A deep inset on an L-shaped plan, where the offset folds over itself; the prototype clamps it. Each needs a small, specific extension: end treatments for runs, per-side profiles for masses, and a safe polygon offset. None of them needs a general modeller, and the proposal is to add them one at a time, only when real creators ask.

Changed

Drawing sections in 3D is unreliable; the lens fixed it

Drawing over a pedestal’s side in perspective works, because the side faces you. Drawing over the section of a frame doesn't: from most views that plane is edge-on. So sections open in a profile lens, a 2D card that shows the real context lines (wall, floor, niche edge) and a 10 cm scale bar. It became the main place for precision and for sharing profiles.

Changed

Tidy has to respect the object’s scale

A 6 cm waist on a 90 cm pedestal side was being straightened away, because the straight-line test compared lengths. It now measures absolute deviation, so gentle intent survives. Tidy needs tuning with real hands on real objects.

Broke

Ties don’t know about collisions

A pedestal that fits the sculpture follows it anywhere, including into a wall. The prototype says so in a note instead of quietly clamping. A niche cannot leave its wall; it stops at the edge, and the note says that too. Deciding how ties should negotiate space is open work.

Changed

A tablet has no hover

The side trace you draw over first appeared on hover. With iPad as the smallest target, the selected form now keeps its trace visible in Draw mode. One finger draws; two fingers look around.

Held

Gravity is not a tie

Assets rest on whatever is under them. Keeping that physical behaviour out of the tie list keeps the list short and meaningful. It now holds only the dependencies a creator chose.

Not built, deliberately

Locks and explicit conflict choices, such as a shelf locked at 1.80 m in a widening room. Variations kept side by side. Lofting between two profiles. Folded sheet forms. All of them fit the model, but none of them was needed to test the core claim.

Scope

What we left out, and why

  • Free-space drawing and arbitrary workplanes. Every surface you draw on comes from the place. The only other plane is the elevation through a form, and it appears only while you draw over that form.
  • Unrestricted booleans. The only subtraction is pushing a plan into its host.
  • Vertex, edge and face editing; B-rep; sculpting. You edit drawings, never geometry.
  • Feature-history trees and node graphs. The recipe sentence and the Relations view show the same dependencies in place, in words.
  • Signed-distance or organic surface modelling. An earlier draft carved forms from two views. It went once the research marked organic forms as asset territory.
  • Fabrication-grade accuracy. Dimensions are true and editable, but nothing here is meant for manufacture.
For engineering

Why it doesn’t break

  • Semantic references. Recipes refer to “the floor seam”, “the right wall” or “Niche’s edge”, never to face indices, so they avoid the topological naming problem that breaks history-based CAD.
  • Declarative recipes, re-made every time. Evaluation runs in a fixed order: the space, then recesses, then masses, then where assets rest, then runs. Meshes are disposable. Undo is a snapshot of the recipe JSON.
  • One kernel. A profiled sweep along a planar path with mitred corners, plus flat caps. Creases are kept from the drawings’ sharp points.
  • Change diffing. Metrics per form (length, height, centre, fitted size) are captured before a change and compared after. The notes are written from those differences and the ties that explain them.
