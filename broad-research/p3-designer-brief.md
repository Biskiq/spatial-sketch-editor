# Designer brief: building spaces that are a pleasure to make and to change

## The product

Biskiq is a browser-native environment for creating, directing and publishing editable spatial
experiences: a pavilion that explains a product, a gallery for a photographer's series, a
walk-through an educator uses to teach a building, an exhibition a storyteller paces room by room.
The creator shapes a world, decides how an audience moves through it and what they discover, and
publishes it as a link anyone can open in a browser.

The value is a *continuing* project. Creators come back, change their mind and revise without
starting again. A change should keep the work they already accepted. It should show them what it
affects and never quietly invent something in its place.

## Who you are designing for

The people who make things in Biskiq are not architects at a CAD workstation. They are product
explainers, educators, photographers and artists, exhibition designers and storytellers. They have
a strong sense of the space they want, such as "a low entrance that opens into something tall, with
light falling from above", but little patience for datums, view ranges, layer panels or modelling
jargon. Some are skilled spatial designers who want precision when it matters. All of them work in
a browser, often on an ordinary laptop, and want to feel in control of a form, not to operate a
machine.

## The design problem

Make expressive spatial creation and revision intuitive, lightweight and browser-first.

Consumer world-builders feel immediate but run out of expressive and vertical range quickly.
Professional tools reach that range at the cost of modes, panels and constant view-switching.
Biskiq needs the immediacy of the first and enough of the range of the second for real
architectural ideas. Above all, the tenth edit must feel as good as the first.

## Challenge 1: Sketch, Build & Sculpt

Let a creator bring a building into being and keep shaping it. This includes walls straight and
curved, round rooms, openings and arches, rooms that emerge from what was drawn, and the overhead
forms that give a space its character: roofs, ceilings, canopies, vaults, folds and lightwells.
"Sculpt" means shaping architecture expressively. It does not mean polygon or clay modelling.

**Representative journeys**

- A photographer sketches a small pavilion freehand: one long curved gallery wall, a round reading
  room, an arched opening between them. They want it to look considered within minutes.
- An exhibition designer lays out a calm, orthogonal set of galleries and concentrates all the
  drama overhead: a raked clerestory roof above a quiet flat ceiling, and a skylight over one room.
- A storyteller builds an outdoor canopy with no walls beneath it, extending past the building's
  footprint to shelter an entrance court.

**Revision scenarios that matter**

- The whole building is too small: make it bigger, then go back and adjust individual rooms
  without losing what was distinctive about them.
- Draw a new wall across an existing room to divide it, then remove it again.
- Reshape a curved wall that already has openings and a room depending on it.
- Change the roof's profile while the ceiling below stays as it was, or the reverse.
- Push a canopy out beyond its original footprint. Edit a roof near a sharp ridge or a tight
  corner.
- Remove something other parts lean on, such as a supporting span or a wall a roof rests on, and
  understand what happens next.

## Challenge 2: Levels & Mezzanine

Let a creator compose vertical space: split elevations, a mezzanine overlooking a double-height
hall, an atrium, a sunken court, a skybridge between two wings, a stair or ramp as an experience in
itself. A real building does not have to share one tidy floor stack. Wings can sit at different
heights, and a platform can straddle two rooms of different ceiling heights.

**Representative journeys**

- An educator builds a two-wing pavilion: an older wing raised on a plinth with tall storeys, and a
  newer wing at grade with lower ones, joined by a bridge.
- An artist composes compression and release: a low approach, a tall interior, a mezzanine to look
  down from, and a lightwell falling past it toward a single sculpture.
- A product explainer places objects on several levels and needs to see and edit a lower room
  while the floors above are in the way.

**Revision scenarios that matter**

- Raise one mezzanine without moving the other wing.
- Change a ceiling height and see what it does to the space above.
- Open an atrium through two floors, then close it partway.
- Move an object from one level to another, or across a cut-away view, without losing it.
- Look inside, peel back or slice through the building, then return easily to exactly where you
  were.

## Connection to spatial storytelling

Architecture in Biskiq exists to be experienced. The same building is later directed, with views,
guided moments, sightlines and pacing, and published for an audience. Your design should leave room
for that afterlife. A cut-away, a reveal or an exploded view that helps the creator understand the
building might also be how a visitor is shown it. That presentation changes how the building is
seen, never the building itself, and none of the creator's working tools leak into what the visitor
sees. Show at least one moment where what was built becomes something told.

## Product constraints (fixed)

- **Browser-first.** The experience runs in an ordinary desktop browser without installs or plug-ins
  and stays responsive on everyday hardware.
- **For non-professionals.** Nothing should require architectural or CAD training to get a good
  result. Precision can be available, but it should not be the price of entry.
- **Meaningful architecture, not raw geometry.** What creators make is architecture: walls,
  openings, rooms, floors, overheads. It is not an arbitrary mesh. Rooms come from what was built;
  creators do not have to declare them before drawing. A round room reads and revises as one
  continuous wall.
- **Honest revision.** A change keeps the work the creator already accepted. Nearness may *suggest*
  a connection, but things become connected only because the creator meant them to. When a change
  affects something else, the creator can see it, and can repair or refuse it. Nothing is silently
  substituted or lost. One action undoes as one step.
- **Inspection is temporary.** Hiding, slicing, ghosting or isolating parts of a building never
  changes the building, and there is always an easy way back.
- **Imported architecture is a legitimate alternative.** Some creators will bring a building
  modelled elsewhere. Native building has to earn its place. Show where it makes creating, revising
  or explaining better, and admit where an external tool is simply better.
- **Not a general modeller.** Deep mesh editing, polygon sculpting, UV work and engineering
  simulation are out of scope.
- **Truthful prototype.** Build it as a standalone, runnable browser prototype, separate from the
  production app. Prepared geometry and simulated behaviour are fine if you say plainly what is
  real, prepared, simulated or unsupported.

## Open design choices (yours)

Everything about *how* is open. That includes:

- how a creator starts: by drawing, gesturing, placing, describing, or something else;
- what they grab, push and shape, and which views they work in;
- how height, levels and overheads are expressed and seen;
- how occlusion is resolved;
- how much of the creator's intent is remembered as the design changes;
- how consequences and repairs are presented;
- what the surface looks like.

You may treat the two challenges as one continuous experience or as two linked ones. You do not
have to follow existing tools, research precedents or the current Biskiq editor. Do not narrow your
idea to forms that are easy to simulate.

Be ambitious and be different. Several designers are receiving this same brief. A distinct,
committed point of view is worth more than a safe average. The only test is whether the person who
wrote this brief enjoys making and remaking a building with what you built.
