The math the gestures hide

Two mechanisms carry most of the synthesis: Kelly & Wonka's per-edge profiles over a rising sweep plane, and PushPull++'s angle-threshold rule for adapting neighbours instead of extruding.

Edge profile (procedural extrusion)

One footprint edge in its local (w, z) frame. θi ∈ [−π/2, π/2] from vertical: θ = 0 is a wall, θ > 0 slopes inward, θ < 0 cantilevers outward.

Angle threshold (PushPull++)

Drag a slanted face fm to a new plane pm. If the neighbour's angle sits within the threshold θ, the neighbour is intersected with pm and its slope adapts; at θ = 0° or beyond the threshold, a new connecting face is inserted.

In exhibition, pavilion, and museum architecture, the upper enclosure is rarely a passive weather barrier; it operates as the primary instrument of spatial choreography, daylight modulation, and emotional pacing1. When visitors traverse an architectural promenade, their proprioceptive and visual comprehension of the space is governed by the dialogue between overhead volume, structural rhythm, and aperture placement1. For Biskiq to function as a standalone creative spatial authoring tool rather than a rigid drafting utility, its native geometry engine must empower creators to author three distinct yet interrelated architectural layers: the exterior roof silhouette, the interior ceiling scenography, and the vertical spatial voids that bind multi-level environments together3. High-ceiling spatial authoring requires transitioning fluidly from room-bound vaults to freestanding canopies and multi-floor light shafts across five foundational overhead typologies: raked clerestory vaults, dual-skin cathedral and coffered shells, folded-plate and hypar cantilevers, multi-level carved light atriums, and porous singularity gridshells4.

Contemporary museum architecture navigates a delicate tension between sculptural expressionism and curatorial utility1. As architectural theorist Larry Shiner and practitioner Richard Scofidio observe, successful gallery architecture must act as a "creative partner" that neither upstages the artwork through intrusive wall geometries—a criticism frequently leveled at the sharply slanted interior walls of Daniel Libeskind's Hamilton Building—nor deadens the visitor experience through sterile, monotonous white boxes, which Renzo Piano notes can "kill works of art as much as hyperactive spaces"1. In buildings such as Diller Scofidio + Renfro's The Broad, Studio Gang's Arkansas Museum of Fine Arts, and Jean Nouvel's Louvre Abu Dhabi, the resolution to this dilemma happens overhead1. By keeping lower exhibition partitions calm and orthogonal while concentrating geometric expressiveness in the roof canopy, ceiling vaults, and circulation atriums, architects achieve monumental spatial drama without compromising wall-mounted curation1.

Architectural BIM and Parametric Shells: Revit, Archicad, and Rhino/Grasshopper

Enterprise BIM platforms treat roofs and ceilings as distinct semantic categories governed by strict planar and parametric rules, requiring users to select a mathematical construction method before exploring form18. In Autodesk Revit, roof authoring is fractured into three incompatible modal workflows: Roof by Footprint, Roof by Extrusion, and Roof by Face18. Roof by Footprint operates in a 2D plan sketch mode where users trace wall perimeters (Pick Walls), specify overhang distances, and toggle a Defines Slope checkbox per boundary edge—expressing pitch as rise-over-run ratios like 6 : 12 or θ = arctan(Rise/Run) ≈ 26.57°—or draw Slope Arrows specifying head-to-tail elevation deltas18. While effective for conventional hipped and gabled roofs, footprint sketches cannot represent curved sectional profiles, and users must manually invoke Attach Top/Base so underlying walls extend up to the roof's underside18. To create barrel vaults or sawtooth factory roofs, users must abandon Footprint mode and switch to Roof by Extrusion, selecting a vertical work plane to sketch an open 2D profile (using lines, arcs, or splines) that is swept linearly between start and end distances18. However, extrusion workflows are restricted strictly to roofs; Revit floors and flat slabs cannot use extrusions, forcing multi-point drainage or faceted slab variations into a cumbersome vertex-elevation Shape Editing (Add Point / Add Split Line) sub-mode18.

When designers require double-curved shells or organic forms in Revit, they must turn to Roof by Face paired with In-Place Mass or Conceptual Mass workflows18. Users first enter a separate massing environment to extrude, blend, sweep, and carve solid masses using Void Forms, and subsequently apply semantic walls, floors, and roofs onto the selected mass faces30. However, empirical workflow research from The American University in Cairo highlights severe usability and geometric failures when bridging freeform NURBS geometry (modeled in Rhinoceros) into Revit's mass-to-roof pipeline27. While 85.7% of designers utilized the Import in Mass method (with only 14.3% importing purely as a reference without conversion), an overwhelming 79.9% encountered geometric import and conversion errors27. Only 37.1% successfully converted masses into native Revit roof and wall elements27. Curvilinear surfaces with high control-point density or complex U/V panelization frequently fail to convert into native Roof by Face elements altogether, forcing 54.2% of users into workaround workflows where irregular shapes are converted into faceted Curtain Systems whose horizontal and vertical mullions collide unpredictably, or requiring tedious back-and-forth loops to reduce control points, re-loft surfaces, and explode masses in Rhino prior to re-import27.

Graphisoft Archicad offers a more unified freeform primitive via its Shell Tool (introduced in Archicad 15), which consolidates thin-shell vaulting into three explicit Geometry Methods: Extruded, Revolved, and Ruled19. Each method requires two geometric inputs—combinations of planar profile curves, extrusion vectors, revolution axes, or pairs of cross-sectional profile polylines22. While the Ruled method allows designers to loft hyperbolic paraboloids and warped shells between two arbitrary 2D profiles, editing a shell after creation requires manipulating abstract profile planes and contour polygons in isolation22. Even when paired with the Grasshopper–Archicad Live Connection, where designers drive native Archicad Shell, Slab, and Beam components via visual scripting nodes, fundamental BIM kernel rigidities persist: Archicad Slab components reject any non-horizontal polygon outside the XY plane, and 3D spline curves passed into Archicad are forcibly segmented into linear polylines22.

At the pinnacle of bespoke museum roof engineering—exemplified by Foster + Partners and Buro Happold's British Museum Great Court Roof or Populous's Aviva Stadium—parametric scripting in GenerativeComponents (GC) or Rhino/Grasshopper replaces direct manipulation entirely8. To span the Great Court's circular central Reading Room (r = a) and its orthogonal outer courtyard walls (x = ±b, y = c, −d) without imparting horizontal lateral thrust onto the historic masonry (which rested on sliding supports), engineer Chris J. K. Williams eschewed standard NURBS surfaces8. Because a smooth NURBS surface without curvature singularities must flatten out horizontally at a right-angled horizontal corner—creating a "ski jump" effect where compressive thrust changes direction rapidly—Williams formulated a blended analytical elevation function combining a level-change function with a conical corner singularity function over which a steel diagrid was relaxed across hundreds of dynamic relaxation cycles8. While mathematically triumphant, requiring creators to formulate boundary-vanishing rational functions or maintain external Excel-driven radial arc scripts (as used in the Aviva Stadium envelope) is antithetical to immediate creative authoring8.

Artist-Centric CAD and Smart Planar Manipulation: SketchUp, PushPull++, Shapr3D, and Plasticity

Direct modeling tools abandon parametric history trees and BIM category restrictions in favor of immediate surface and solid manipulation33. Trimble SketchUp democratized 3D architectural modeling through its signature Push/Pull interaction, allowing users to extrude any closed 2D planar face along its normal vector44. However, native SketchUp fails rapidly when confronting sloped, intersecting, or curved roofs: pushing a slanted roof plane either extrudes an unwanted orthogonal step or breaks the planarity of adjacent wall and roof facets, degenerating quads into fractured triangles24. Furthermore, SketchUp lacks native semantic awareness—surfaces are treated as unclassified meshes unless manually tagged44—and requires third-party extensions like Fredo6's Curviloft (offering Loft by Spline, Loft along Path, and Skinning contour loops) and Joint Push Pull (to thicken curved skins into volumetric shells) to construct organic roofs or tensile canopies28.

To overcome the fragility of standard push-pull on architectural polyhedra, Markus Lipp, Peter Wonka, and colleagues introduced PushPull++ (subsequently integrated into Esri CityEngine workflows), which reduces the complexity of common architectural modeling tasks by up to an order of magnitude24. PushPull++ resolves the three core failure modes of planar architectural editing through unified mathematical rules24. First, to decide whether dragging a slanted face fm to a target plane pm should slide existing adjacent slopes or insert new extruded side walls, the algorithm evaluates the angle between adjacent faces in the counterclockwise fan Ffan around each vertex v against an angle threshold θ24. If the angle sits within the threshold (such as θ = 30° or θ = 70°), existing adjacent planes are intersected directly with pm to adapt the roof slope smoothly; if θ = 0° or the angle exceeds the threshold, new connecting faces are dynamically inserted, reducing to a traditional extrusion24. Second, rather than moving individual vertices—which warps adjacent quad faces into non-planar skew polygons—PushPull++ maintains strict face planarity by computing new vertex positions exclusively through the intersection of triples of adjacent face planes with pm24. Third, when dragging a roof plane or wall causes an adjacent edge or face to collapse to zero length or self-intersect, standard CAD kernels either fail or invoke expensive Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG) booleans24. PushPull++ detects these conflict events—termed Topological Change Points (TCPs)—along the drag trajectory τ and executes a stepwise modification, decomposing a single user drag into a continuous sequence of intermediate plane updates across each τ ± ε boundary to preserve a valid two-manifold solid throughout the gesture24.

In industrial design CAD, Shapr3D and Plasticity demonstrate how Parasolid B-rep and NURBS kernels can be stripped of engineering clutter33. Shapr3D pairs pen-first 2D sketching (with automatic constraint inferencing) with direct 3D face pushing: dragging a profile into a solid cuts a void, dragging outward extrudes a volume, and dragging an on-screen draft-angle glyph tilts the extrusion into a sloped hip or pyramid43. For curved roofs, Shapr3D and Fusion 360 utilize Path + Guide Rail sweeps and multi-profile Lofts, where dragging curvature handles at control points dynamically adjusts surface tension while live Design History updates downstream shells and fillets54. Plasticity ("CAD for artists") goes further by combining a Parasolid NURBS kernel with Blender's modal keyboard ergonomics (E for Extrude with angle/thickness, L for Loft, Shift+P for Sweep, P for Pipe booleans)33. In Plasticity, creators do not need to enter a separate edit mode to shape splines; clicking and dragging control points directly deforms curves, holding Alt proportionally deforms a curve up to the next vertex, and the Patch Hole tool can close an open top boundary while conforming to an interior convex guide curve (powered by variational surfacing via xNURBS)33. Despite their speed, both Shapr3D and Plasticity remain general-purpose object modelers: they lack architectural semantics (levels, walls auto-trimming to roofs, or room shells) and require high manual dexterity with 3D curves and keyboard shortcuts51.

Procedural Extrusions and Straight Skeletons: Kelly & Wonka, Medeek, and Dual Graphs

When a creator draws an arbitrary 2D room footprint—such as an L-shaped, U-shaped, or multi-courtyard gallery—computing how pitched or profiled roof planes meet at hips, valleys, and ridges is governed by the Straight Skeleton algorithm (Aichholzer et al., 1995)60. Defined by shrinking all polygon edges inward parallel to themselves at a constant speed, the straight skeleton projects the 3D roof ridges and hips onto the 2D base plane as a loop-free tree structure, coinciding with the Voronoi diagram for convex polygons60.

While basic straight skeletons assume every roof plane shares an identical constant pitch and equal eave height62, Tom Kelly and Peter Wonka's seminal framework, Interactive Architectural Modeling with Procedural Extrusions (2011), expanded the weighted straight skeleton into a general-purpose interactive architectural shell generator21. In Kelly and Wonka's system, each edge of a 2D floorplan is assigned a local 2D polyline profile in a (w, z) coordinate system, defining a sequence of m − 1 inclination angles θi ∈ [−π/2, π/2] relative to the vertical plane, where θ = 0 produces a vertical wall, θ > 0 slopes inward, and θ < 0 cantilevers outward21. A horizontal sweep plane rises vertically from the base of the building, maintaining an evolving 2D cross-section called the active plan (stored as a doubly linked list of corners and edges) and resolving wavefront collision events in a priority queue21. Curved roofs, mansards, and overhangs are authored simply by adding vertices to the 2D profile polyline; an overhang is inserted as an atomic UI operation that injects a horizontal outward step into the profile at a specified elevation21. Dormers, chimneys, buttresses, and skylights are authored via discrete plan edits anchored to the profile: forced steps inject arbitrary user-drawn edges into the rising active plan, while natural steps insert parametric shapes (like a rectangular dormer box or exterior buttress) that seamlessly merge their own roof profiles into the parent roof's wavefront21.

Recent procedural reconstruction research—such as ShellMaker, Medeek's weighted straight skeleton framing solvers, and dual-graph roof synthesis—highlights an important limitation of pure straight-skeleton methods: standard and weighted straight skeletons struggle when a single planar roof face must span multiple non-collinear outline edges, or when Asian and contemporary architectural styles require gable ends and multi-tier eaves that violate uniform wavefront shrinking10. Encoding roof topology as a Dual Graph atop the primal roof graph—or allowing creators to interactively flip individual footprint edges between hip (sloped) and gable (vertical) states—resolves these topological ambiguities while preserving clean eave, ridge, and hip line classifications10.

Cozy World-Builders and Game Construction Systems: Tiny Glade, The Sims 4, Townscaper, Paralives, and Enshrouded

Consumer building games and diorama tools achieve extraordinary approachability by inverting the CAD workflow: creators manipulate high-level spatial blocks or strokes while a procedural "chemistry" engine synthesizes valid architectural joints, trims, and details in real time66. Tiny Glade exemplifies "gridless building chemistry"66. Rather than requiring users to select a dedicated roof tool, placing a rectangular, circular, or freehand wall enclosure automatically generates a procedural roof67. Right-clicking any structure enters Focus Mode, exposing direct-manipulation 3D gizmos in the viewport to drag roof pitch height, slide eave overhangs, adjust foundation stone height, or bend the roof profile from a concave medieval swoop to a pointed gothic arch or conical spire70. Crucially, when two roofed volumes are dragged into overlapping proximity, Tiny Glade dynamically computes the boolean intersection, carving clean valley rafters, merging ridge lines, or converting a smaller intersecting volume into a gabled dormer or archway automatically67. To prevent viewport clutter—a notorious failure mode of 3D handle systems—Tiny Glade implements focus-mode gizmo hygiene: when manipulating small shapes or vertices, non-essential UI gizmos automatically fade away based on camera proximity and active drag state, allowing 1 mm placement precision without visual occlusion71. Furthermore, structural columns or building supports are not placed manually; they generate automatically under overhangs and stairs and can be selectively knocked out per segment with a Hammer Tool or restored in Focus Mode70.

The Sims 4 Build Mode decouples roofs from room generation, treating roofs as independent, stretchable parametric primitives (Gabled, Hipped, Half-Gabled, Half-Hipped, Pagoda, Octagonal, Circular) placed atop or alongside rooms20. Selecting a roof reveals three sets of 3D viewport handles: footprint sizing arrows (pulling the roof box), eave flaring triangles (extending overhangs independently per side or symmetrically), and a vertical pitch sphere20. Dragging the slope curve sphere bends the entire roof plane into a uniform convex barrel or concave pagoda curve20. Crucially, pressing the hidden power-user shortcut Shift + C while a roof is selected unlocks additional intermediate curvature control nodes along the slope profile, allowing creators to bend a single roof into multi-inflection S-curves, mansard breaks, or flared eaves20. While highly expressive for exteriors, The Sims 4's roof system suffers from two major architectural flaws for an interior museum editor: roof primitives remain flat or culled on the underside, failing to generate matching vaulted interior ceilings, and overlapping multiple roof primitives to create L-shaped or cross-gable roofs causes intersecting roof tails to clip visibly into upper-floor rooms unless complex room-culling rules trigger properly.

At the extreme pole of zero-parameter procedural inference, Oskar Stålberg's Townscaper operates on an irregular relaxed grid where the user's sole interaction is clicking to add or remove blocks69. A tile-based procedural generator inspects vertical and horizontal neighbor adjacency to automatically turn top blocks into pitched roofs, merge adjacent towers under a continuous gable, or convert a spanning roof block over a gap into an automatic archway69. While magical as an expressive toy, Townscaper offers zero direct control over roof pitch, ceiling height, or interior volume76. Meanwhile, Paralives bridges the gap between The Sims and freeform drafting by introducing gridless, arbitrary-angle, and curved wall drawing with full N-gon room recognition and split-level platforms, though adapting procedural roofs and window openings onto curved walls remains a complex frontier requiring dedicated topological solvers78. Finally, Enshrouded demonstrates the capabilities and limits of sub-meter voxel building80. Builders construct grand halls and domed ceilings using voxel blocks and templates that automatically apply surface smoothing and contextual edge decorations81. While voxels allow arbitrary subterranean carving84, creators frequently fight the rigid 90° world grid—unable to angle building pieces off-grid—and encounter frustrating procedural smoothing artifacts, such as window arches smoothing differently on the north versus east side of a building due to directional voxel rules, or roof edge details disappearing when terrain voxels sit too close81.

Volumetric Implicit Sculpting and Sketch-Based Lifting: Womp 3D, MagicaCSG, and SmartCanvas

Adjacent creative disciplines bypass polygonal topology and CAD boundary representations (B-reps) entirely by representing shapes as Signed Distance Fields (SDFs) or lifted 2D perspective strokes31. In Womp 3D and MagicaCSG (as well as Unreal and Unity distance-field modelers like Clayxels and MudBun), creators assemble primitive mathematical volumes—spheres, cylinders, rounded boxes, and multi-point curves—that are evaluated in real time via hardware-independent raymarching31. Every primitive can be toggled between Positive form (additive) and Negative form (subtractive boolean carving)31. Womp's signature interaction is the Goop slider: within an upward-influencing scene-list hierarchy, a modifying object smoothly blends into any base object positioned above it in the layer stack, creating watertight liquid fillets, organic vaulted cavities, and continuous material gradients at zero boolean mesh cost31. Negative-form primitives with high Goop values act as expanding soft cutters, carving smooth, cavernous domes, ocular skylights, and Gilder-Center-style organic atriums out of solid architectural masses9. Furthermore, recent advances in neural SDF stroke-based editing allow artists to draw directly onto 3D implicit volumes with custom 2D brush profiles, supporting chiseled strokes, asymmetric shapes, and simultaneous carving and extrusion89. The primary limitation of pure SDF modeling for architecture is the absence of explicit planar faces, crisp vertices, and lightweight polygon topology: moving an individual roof corner is impossible because vertices do not exist, and heavy domain warping degrades distance-field accuracy, causing rendering artifacts or requiring dense polygonization (via Dual Contouring) for real-time engine playback31.

Academic sketch-based interfaces—spanning Teddy (chordal-axis inflation)92, SmartCanvas (lifting 2D freehand strokes into 3D "sweeping canvases" by inferring vanishing directions, coplanarity, and hinge junctions)86, and DreamSketch, Neural Canvas, and Womp Spark (conversational and multimodal sketch-to-3D generation)93—demonstrate that 2D strokes remain the most immediate medium for early spatial ideation34. However, as evaluated in the EditHero benchmark for long-horizon 3D editing, top-down generative AI ("vibe modeling") methods that regenerate latent 3D representations on each turn fail across multi-step architectural revisions, missing requested edits and mutating untouched parts of the asset across edit chains96. Bottom-up, part-aware programmatic or procedural operations preserve untouched spatial context with far greater consistency across long sequences of iterative revisions96.

3. Competing Design Directions for Biskiq

To determine how Biskiq should empower non-specialists to author museum-grade spatial volumes, the analysis evaluates four substantially different design directions. Each direction is grounded in a distinct geometric philosophy and tested against concrete exhibition design scenarios.

Direction 1: Tactile Spine & Eave Chemistry (Enhanced Procedural Extrusion)

The first design direction treats every enclosed room, corridor, or drawn footprint as the seed for an intelligent, watertight architectural roof-ceiling shell governed by a real-time weighted straight skeleton and vertical sweep-plane solver21. Rather than drafting 2D CAD profiles in a separate window, creators sculpt the roof's 3D form directly in the viewport using an evolved version of Tiny Glade's Focus Mode gizmos and The Sims 4's Shift + C profile handles20. Drawing or selecting any room—whether orthogonal, N-gon, or bounded by curved walls—exposes a primary Apex/Ridge Handle at the topological center or spine of the straight skeleton alongside Eave Handles on each boundary segment71. Clicking an eave edge or pulling its gable toggle handle flips its direction plane angle to θ = 0 (vertical wall), instantly converting a hipped end into a gable wall, a single-slope raked roof (when three edges are flattened), or a cross-gable layout18. Dragging a midpoint sphere along any roof slope bends the straight rafter line into a smooth spline curve, producing a convex barrel vault, concave pagoda, or ogee arch20. Double-clicking the slope line inserts a Profile Inflection Node—directly translating Kelly and Wonka's polyline profile vertices into an on-model 3D widget—to author mansard breaks, gambrel roofs, stepped ziggurats, or cantilevered overhangs11. Furthermore, dragging a secondary room or roof volume into an existing roof automatically resolves the intersection via the sweep-plane solver, transforming the smaller intersecting roof into a clean dormer, cross-vault, or skylight well without interior geometry clipping21.

In a concrete creator scenario for a daylight-raked sculpture gallery, a creator builds a 15 m × 25 m rectangular gallery, right-clicks the roof to enter Focus Mode, clicks the north, east, and west eaves to lock them as vertical walls (θ = 0), and drags the north eave upward by 3.5 m. The roof immediately becomes a single-slope skillion vault raking upward toward the north garden4. As the north wall stretches vertically to meet the raised eave, a contextual prompt offers a single-click action to insert a clerestory band, automatically framing a continuous ribbon of glazing along the upper trapezoid of the north wall4.

Direction 2: The Architectural Ribcage (Guided Lofting & Sweep Canvases)

Expressive museum roofs—such as undulating wave canopies, vaulted basilica naves, and folded-plate pavilions—rarely conform to uniform inward-shrinking footprints6. The second design direction treats upper enclosures as a structural ribcage of 2D sectional arches or polylines swept along or lofted across spatial guide rails, combining the power of Archicad's Shell Tool, Plasticity's Loft and Patch workflows, and SmartCanvas's 3D sketch planes25. Creators place or draw vertical Sweep Canvases (cross-sectional planes) across a space or freestanding courtyard, sketching a 2D profile—such as a pointed Gothic arch, an asymmetrical wave, a pleated zig-zag line, or a semicircular barrel arc—directly onto the plane18. Selecting two or more ribs, or dragging a rib along a floor path, lofts a continuous 3D architectural shell across them46. Creators can toggle between Smooth Spline Lofting for organic waves and Ruled or Folded Interpolation for Félix Candela hypar shells and Studio Gang folded-plate concrete canopies6. Additionally, selecting an outer perimeter wall loop and an inner courtyard ring invokes a Boundary Patch Skin with adjustable mid-span inflation and corner tension, replicating the British Museum Great Court roof in two clicks8, while any wall beneath a Ribcage Shell can be toggled to trim dynamically to the undulating underside of the lofted surface18.

In a concrete creator scenario for a freestanding folded-plate pavilion uniting two museum wings, a creator works without drawing any walls by sketching three parallel sectional canvases across a plaza9. They draw a four-peak pleated zig-zag line on the first canvas, duplicate it to the third, and scale the middle canvas's zig-zag taller and wider. Selecting the three ribs generates a self-supporting folded-plate concrete canopy, and a Tiny-Glade-style contextual toggle spawns procedural V-columns at the outer valley vertices6.

Direction 3: Implicit Volumetric Clay & Subtractive Light-Carving (SDF Massing & Booleans)

Inspired by Studio Gang's cavernous Gilder Center, TEAM_BLDG's Songzhuang Z Museum atrium, and the liquid SDF workflows of Womp 3D and MagicaCSG, the third design direction conceives of architecture not as thin assembled planes, but as carved poetic mass5. Creators block out positive exterior building masses and carve interior vaulted halls, light wells, and multi-story atriums using subtractive negative volumes with adjustable blending ("Goop") radii30. By stacking positive masses and negative Interior Void primitives in a unified scene hierarchy, creators carve playable interior space directly out of solid architectural blocks30. Each void or roof mass features an Edge Blend (Goop) Slider: setting the blend to zero yields crisp, orthogonal modernist galleries or sharp coffered ceiling recesses4, whereas increasing the blend radius smoothly melts intersecting cylinders and spheres into groin vaults, pendentive domes, or sculptural canyon-like atriums where walls flow seamlessly into arched ceilings9. Furthermore, creators can drag cylindrical or conical Light Cutters from the roof exterior diagonally down through multiple floor slabs; where the cutter pierces the exterior shell, Biskiq automatically generates a glazed skylight or ocular frame, and where it slices interior floor slabs, it generates atrium parapet railings or structural openings5.

In a concrete creator scenario for a cavernous multi-story atrium inside a four-story brutalist museum block, a creator drops three overlapping negative capsule volumes spanning from Level 1 to the roof, increasing their blend strength until they merge into a single branching, canyon-like central void reminiscent of the Gilder Center9. Where the upper lobe breaches the flat roof slab, Biskiq converts the intersection cap into a curved glass skylight, flooding all four gallery tiers with diffused top-light5.

Direction 4: Dual-Skin Envelope & Interior Scenographic Liner

In sophisticated museum architecture, the exterior roof weather-shield and the interior gallery ceiling are frequently decoupled systems separated by an interstitial plenum for lighting, structure, and daylight diffusion—as seen in top-lit louver roofs, Jean Nouvel's perforated dome over pavilions, or renovations that conceal vaulted or coffered liners beneath industrial trusses3. The fourth design direction formalizes this separation by giving every spatial volume an Outer Roof Envelope and an independently sculptable Inner Ceiling Liner7. By default, a roof's interior ceiling mirrors its exterior pitch as a coupled cathedral assembly with uniform thickness7. Clicking an Unlink Ceiling Liner toggle splits the shell into two independent control surfaces17. Beneath a simple flat or gabled exterior roof, a creator can pull the inner ceiling liner downward into a shallow scissor-truss vault, curve it into an acoustic barrel vault, carve a recessed Coffered Grid with automatic perimeter LED strip channels, or suspend floating acoustic and luminous clouds that compress ceiling height over specific exhibits while leaving the raw structural roof visible around the perimeter4. If the outer roof is glazed with skylights and the inner ceiling liner is perforated with coffers, louvers, or ocular cutouts, natural light bounces through the interstitial plenum before entering the gallery, giving creators authentic museum daylight modulation3.

4. High-Ceiling Synthesis: The Unified Biskiq Spatial Authoring Architecture

A critical insight from the comparative analysis is that Biskiq should not force creators to choose between a "Footprint Roof Tool," a "Lofted Shell Tool," a "Ceiling Grid Tool," and an "Atrium Cutout Tool"18. In Revit and Archicad, modal tool proliferation forces the creator to anticipate the final mathematical geometry before making their first mark—if a creator starts with a Roof by Footprint and later decides to curve one bay into a barrel vault or extend an open folded-plate canopy over the courtyard, they must delete the roof and rebuild it from scratch using Roof by Extrusion or In-Place Massing18. Instead, Biskiq can achieve an exceptionally high creative ceiling from a small, unified interaction vocabulary by synthesizing the four directions into a single progressive-disclosure architecture: The Elastic Architectural Shell. Under the hood, Biskiq represents every overhead enclosure—whether generated automatically over a room or drawn freely in space as a pavilion canopy—as a Dual-Surface Parametric Shell (𝒮 = {Σext, Σint, ℬ}), where Σext is the exterior roof surface, Σint is the interior ceiling surface, and ℬ is the perimeter boundary loop, which may be bound to wall tops, partially supported by columns, or completely freestanding17. By unifying the data model, every shell in Biskiq responds to the exact same four-gesture interaction vocabulary.

A primary reason non-specialists abandon 3D modeling tools is destructive revision: changing a room's width or moving a wall breaks the roof above it, causing non-planar polygon tearing or requiring manual rebuilding24. Biskiq's synthesized architecture preserves authored intent across iterative revisions through three topological invariants. First, adopting the analytical plane-binding logic proven in structural BIM kernels (such as LIRA-CAD's associative plane trimming) and PushPull++24, all interior and exterior walls beneath a shell maintain an associative top-bound link to Σint18. Moving a wall laterally, curving a wall segment, or raising a roof's pitch automatically recalculates the wall-to-ceiling intersection curve in real time18. If an interior partition intersects a load-bearing perimeter wall under a sloped vault, a strict priority hierarchy ensures the partition trims cleanly against the perimeter wall and sloped ceiling without poking through the exterior roof skin99. Second, as demonstrated in semantic LoD3 building synchronization and PushPull++, when a creator deforms a roof slope, inserts a dormer step, or carves a skylight opening, Biskiq preserves surface materials, coffer grids, and semantic classifications by tracking faces in a topological tree and re-sampling UVs aligned to each roof facet's longest eave or ridge vector44. Third, if a creator modifies the 2D wall footprint of a room whose roof has been sculpted with custom cross-sectional ribs, Biskiq proportionally re-anchors the guide ribs to the normalized [0, 1] bounding coordinates of the updated footprint—similar to Plasticity's Alt-proportional curve deformation—preserving the authored curvature profile without resetting the roof to a flat default21.

Rather than constructing a monolithic geometry kernel upfront, the Biskiq product and engineering team can validate the core interaction hypotheses and resolve the most acute UX tradeoffs through four focused, lightweight interactive prototypes.

In Kelly and Wonka's procedural extrusion interface, users edit roof cross-sections in a dedicated 2D (w, z) profile sub-widget, which provides mathematical clarity for complex overhangs and stepped cornices but splits visual attention away from the 3D scene21. Conversely, The Sims 4 (Shift + C) and Tiny Glade keep all handles strictly on the 3D roof mesh, which feels immediate but can cause handle crowding on small dormers or acute camera angles20. Prototype 1 should test a Hybrid Contextual Ribbon: dragging a 3D handle on a roof slope bends or pitches the 3D mesh directly, while holding a modifier key (or pausing the drag) projects a faint, translucent 2D sectional profile grid directly onto the 3D cutting plane at the cursor's location, combining SmartCanvas's in-situ 3D plane feedback with Tiny Glade's distance-based gizmo fading21.

Straight skeletons excel at room-bound roofs but cannot express a single continuous wave or folded plate that cantilevers across indoor and outdoor zones9. Prototype 2 should evaluate the Upgrade-to-Ribcage transition: a creator begins with a standard room roof, draws a single zig-zag or curved stroke across the roof's front edge, and pulls that edge out over an adjacent courtyard. The experiment will measure whether creators intuitively grasp how two or three cross-sectional ribs control the lofted surface between them, and how effectively a single three-way toggle (Smooth Spline, Ruled Hypar, and Faceted Folded Plate) communicates the resulting architectural geometry6.

Because Biskiq has already established foundational patterns for architectural sections, cutaways, and ceiling lifting, Prototype 3 should test how upper-enclosure authoring interfaces with those view modes. When a ceiling is lifted or a building is viewed from above with top-down backface culling, creators cannot see the interior ceiling they are trying to vault or coffer. Prototype 3 should test an Automatic Dollhouse Cross-Section Focus: when a creator selects the Interior Ceiling Liner handle, Biskiq smoothly slides a temporary vertical section plane to bisect the room, rendering the outer roof (Σext) as a semi-transparent ghost envelope while presenting the inner ceiling (Σint) as a solid, tactile surface that can be pulled down into a barrel vault, raked toward a clerestory, or subdivided into illuminated coffers4.

To test whether Womp 3D's liquid SDF modeling can coexist with crisp planar architecture85, Prototype 4 should implement a Scoped Volumetric Cutter. Rather than converting the entire museum into an SDF—which would round off sharp exterior corners and degrade wall planarity where artworks hang1—the museum's primary walls, slabs, and outer roofs remain crisp planar and NURBS shells, while multi-level Atrium Void Cutters evaluate locally as implicit distance fields within their bounding box, using Dual Contouring (which preserves sharp features alongside smooth blends) to stitch the carved atrium lining seamlessly into the floor slabs and skylight openings5.

The friction that plagues existing 3D architectural tools stems from a false dichotomy: consumer games such as The Sims 4 and Townscaper assume creators only care about exterior dollhouse silhouettes, while professional CAD and BIM suites such as Revit, Archicad, and Rhino assume creators are engineers willing to manage modal sketch planes, U/V NURBS control-point topologies, and manual wall-trimming constraints18.

For Biskiq to stand as the premier creative spatial editor for museums, galleries, and expressive 3D environments, its roof, ceiling, and volumetric authoring experience must unite three foundational principles into a cohesive creative flow. First, the editor must be space-first rather than object-first: creators in Biskiq are ultimately sculpting interior volume, compression and release, and natural daylight rather than mere exterior roof hats2. By treating every overhead enclosure as a Dual-Skin Elastic Shell that couples an exterior weather canopy with an unzippable interior ceiling liner, Biskiq allows a creator to effortlessly pair a dramatic cantilevered exterior silhouette with an intimate barrel-vaulted or coffered interior gallery4. Second, the workflow must offer progressive disclosure without dead ends so that a creator never has to delete a roof to change its geometric class18. Every upper enclosure should begin with zero-friction straight-skeleton chemistry, bend into vaults or raked clerestories via direct 3D slope handles, upgrade seamlessly into multi-rib folded plates or wave canopies when cross-sectional ribs are added, and accept subtractive multi-floor atrium light-shafts at any stage of iteration5. Finally, by combining PushPull++ stepwise planar adaptation with associative wall-to-ceiling trimming and semantic opening synthesis, every creative drag gesture in Biskiq is guaranteed to produce self-healing, watertight, two-manifold architecture—automatically extending walls to meet undulating vaults, framing clerestory windows into raked elevation gaps, and wrapping carved floor voids into luminous museum atriums5.
